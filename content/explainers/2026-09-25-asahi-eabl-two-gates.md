## What's going on here?

Diageo agreed in December 2025 to sell its controlling stake in East African Breweries (EABL) to Japan's Asahi Group, a deal reported at about US$2.3 billion and described by Diageo as part of a plan to reduce its debt.

On 11 September 2026 the Competition Authority of Kenya (CAK) approved the deal. UDV (Kenya) was cleared without conditions. Diageo Kenya was cleared on two: at least 20% of the refrigeration space it supplies to retail outlets must stay open to rival beer and cider brands, and part of the sale proceeds must be set aside to settle outstanding liabilities before the deal completes.

## Why does a competition authority care about fridges?

In beer, the fridge is the shelf. Brewers lend coolers to bars and shops, and a cooler full of one company's brands is a cooler that rivals cannot get into. The CAK's condition keeps a fifth of that space open, with carve-outs for supermarkets, fuel-station liquor stores, upmarket bars and hotels above two stars, where the concern is weaker.

This is a behavioural remedy: a condition on how the merged business conducts itself after completion. A structural remedy would require it to sell a business or assets. Behavioural remedies are cheaper for the buyer and harder for the regulator to police, which is why they usually come with reporting obligations.

## What is a mandatory offer, and why does an exemption matter?

> ### What is a mandatory offer?
> When a buyer acquires effective control of a company listed in Nairobi, the Capital Markets (Take-overs and Mergers) Regulations, 2002 generally require it to offer to buy every other shareholder's shares on the same terms. The Capital Markets Authority (CMA) can exempt a buyer from that duty, as it did in June for Vodafone Kenya on Safaricom under regulation 5(1) of those Regulations.

The CMA exempted Asahi from making a mandatory offer to EABL's minority shareholders. A group of those shareholders appealed to the Capital Markets Tribunal, arguing they should have had the chance to sell at the deal price. That appeal is still pending.

## What did the High Court do?

On 2 September, Justice Francis Gikonyo ordered that the ownership and control of EABL as at 18 June 2026 stay unchanged until the tribunal decides the appeal and the CAK completes its review. The petition, brought by a shareholder, raised disclosure, minority protection and the history of Diageo's own 2022 and 2023 tender offer, which took it from about 50% to 65%.

The CAK has now finished its review. On the court's own terms, completion turns on the tribunal.

## Who still has to say yes?

- The Capital Markets Tribunal, on the minority shareholders' appeal.
- The High Court, whose status quo order stays in place until that appeal is decided.
- Any other regulator whose consent the sale agreement makes a condition, which the parties have not published.

## What would the lawyers be doing?

On a deal like this the corporate team drafts conditions precedent and a long-stop date that can absorb a tribunal timetable, the competition team negotiates the fridge-space condition and the reporting that goes with it, and the disputes team runs the petition and the appeal. Junior lawyers keep the approvals tracker, prepare the regulatory filings and build the disclosure record that a minority challenge will test.

## What bigger trend does this show?

In June the Government's sale of 15% of Safaricom to Vodacom went through the same sequence in days: a conservatory order, a Court of Appeal stay, a CMA exemption and a block trade. EABL shows the other outcome, where the exemption becomes the pressure point and a single shareholder can hold the deal at the status quo. Buyers of listed Kenyan companies should now plan for both.
