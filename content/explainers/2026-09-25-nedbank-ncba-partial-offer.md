## The offer in brief

On 21 January 2026 Nedbank Group served notice on NCBA Group's board, the Capital Markets Authority, the Nairobi Securities Exchange and the Competition Authority of Kenya that it intended to acquire about 66% of NCBA, or 1,087,362,891 shares. NCBA is one of East Africa's larger financial groups, with banks in Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Rwanda, Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana, 122 branches and about KSh 665 billion of assets.

The price was KSh 105 for each NCBA share, which valued the bank at about 1.4 times book value. Nedbank paid it in two parts: KSh 21 in cash and 0.0402994 new Nedbank shares for each NCBA share tendered, so that a shareholder tendering 100 shares received KSh 2,100 and about four Nedbank shares. Holders whose entitlement would have come to fewer than 200 Nedbank shares were offered KSh 105 a share entirely in cash, which spared small investors from holding a handful of shares listed in Johannesburg.

## Why a partial offer needs an exemption

> ### What is a partial offer?
> A partial offer is a take-over offer for less than all of a company's shares, made to every shareholder on the same terms. Each shareholder can sell the same proportion of their holding, so the buyer gains control while the company stays listed with a public minority.

Kenya's take-over rules start from the position that a person who acquires effective control of a listed company must offer to buy all of its shares, so that every shareholder can leave at the price paid for control. A partial offer departs from that starting point, which is why Nedbank needed the Capital Markets Authority's permission. The Authority exempted Nedbank from making a mandatory offer for all NCBA shares on 19 February 2026. By then, holders of 77.54% of NCBA had given irrevocable undertakings to accept.

The partial offer answers the concern behind the mandatory offer rule in its own way. Every shareholder is offered the same price for the same proportion of their shares, so no one is excluded from the control premium, and the bank stays listed with about 34% of its shares in public hands. The Asahi and EABL transaction (No. 1 of 2026) took a different route: control is passing through a holding company, the minority have no offer at all, and the exemption is now before the Capital Markets Tribunal.

## How the scaling works

The offer ran from 28 May to 10 July 2026. Each shareholder was entitled to tender 66% of their holding and could apply to sell more. When the offer closed, acceptances covered about 79.9% of NCBA's shares: 920.65 million shares, or 55.88%, under the pro rata entitlement, and a further 395.71 million, or 24.02%, as excess applications.

Nedbank is buying exactly 66%, so the excess applications fill the gap between the pro rata tenders and the target. On the published figures, that gap is about 166.7 million shares out of the 395.7 million offered as excess, which means that excess applications will be met at roughly 42% and the rest returned. Shareholders who tendered only their entitlement will sell all of it.

## Nine regulators, three regions

A bank with subsidiaries in six countries, bought by a bank in a seventh, needs approvals in each of them. The competition clearances came from the COMESA Competition Commission, the East African Community Competition Authority, Tanzania's Fair Competition Commission and the ECOWAS Regional Competition Authority. The banking approvals came from the Prudential Authority and the Financial Surveillance Department of the South African Reserve Bank, the National Bank of Rwanda, the Bank of Tanzania and, on 28 August 2026, the Central Bank of Kenya, which approved the acquisition under section 13(4) of the Banking Act. The Central Bank said that the deal would support stability, strengthen the resilience of Kenyan banking and promote competition.

## What shareholders receive

Nedbank expects to issue about 43.6 million new shares, about 8.4% of its ordinary shares after the transaction, and to pay about R3.0 billion in cash, roughly KSh 23 billion. Settlement follows completion, which Nedbank has kept at the end of the third quarter or early in the fourth quarter of 2026, and the final results of the offer are due on the tenth trading day after settlement. Kenyan shareholders who accepted will then hold Nedbank shares alongside the NCBA shares they kept, and should check with their brokers how those Johannesburg-listed shares can be held and sold.

## What it means for NCBA and for Kenyan banking

NCBA stays listed on the Nairobi Securities Exchange, with its brand and its local management in place and Nedbank as a 66% shareholder. For Nedbank, the deal positions it for expansion across East Africa, and the NCBA contribution is one of the drivers behind the medium-term return targets it set out with its half-year results.

For Kenyan banking, the deal brings a large South African group into a sector that is consolidating as the minimum core capital requirement for banks rises in stages to KSh 10 billion by 2029 under the Business Laws (Amendment) Act, 2024. It also leaves a template for future control deals on the NSE: a partial offer made to every shareholder on the same terms, with irrevocable undertakings secured before launch and the regulators lined up jurisdiction by jurisdiction.
